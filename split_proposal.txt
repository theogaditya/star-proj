1. Rationale for the Split
The original paper has two distinct and independently valuable scientific contributions that are
currently co-located:
Contribution 1 (Diagnostic): A systematic empirical characterization of why CPU-based
HPA fails for stateful WebSocket workloads, using a progressive chain of four controlled
experiments (A, B1, B2-Instrumented, B3). This contribution stands entirely on its own —
the failure modes it documents are real, quantified, and actionable regardless of what
solution follows.

Contribution 2 (Solution + Evaluation): The design, implementation, and multi-baseline
experimental validation of a custom Kubernetes controller ( StatefulAutoscaler ) that
resolves the documented failure modes, including adversarial scenario analysis, protocol
generalization to MQTT, and comparison against HPA+custom-metric and KEDA.
Why this split is clean:
Paper 1 requires zero knowledge of the StatefulAutoscaler . Its conclusions (failure
modes, root causes, quantified metrics) are self-contained.
Paper 2 requires only a summary of the failure modes from Paper 1 as motivating context; it
does not need to reproduce the full experimental evidence chain.
Both papers share the same infrastructure, background, and related work, but can adapt
those sections independently to their own framing.
Target venues: Both papers target the same journal tier (e.g., Springer journals in distributed
systems/cloud computing). Paper 1 is a measurement/characterization paper; Paper 2 is a
systems/design paper.

Subsection 5.1: CPU as an Invalid Scaling Signal for Stateful Workloads
Reproduce argument from original: CPU is not merely suboptimal — it is structurally
invalid.
The signal drops precisely because connections are idle → HPA destroys the connections →
circular destruction.
Experiment C result (CPU_WORK=0, correct scaling) is cited here as evidence from the
companion paper (Paper 2) that a correct signal on the same control structure produces
correct behavior.
Key phrasing: "a perfect proportional controller on the wrong signal produces worse
outcomes than a simpler controller on the correct signal."
Subsection 5.2: The Stabilization Window Semantic Mismatch
Explain why tuning HPA's stabilization window cannot fix the problem.
HPA window buffers metric-derived replica recommendations. With persistent near-zero
CPU, every window entry recommends minReplicas → stabilized output = minReplicas.
The correct window must buffer connection-derived desired counts — retaining the
memory that 8 replicas were required even when connections transiently drop to zero.
Conclude: "This is not a tuning problem; it is a semantic mismatch between what the
window stabilizes."

Forward pointer: "The companion paper (Paper 2) implements and evaluates a controller
with this corrected stabilization semantic."
Subsection 5.3: The 30-Second Limbo Window as a Design Constraint
Formalize the SIGTERM→SIGKILL sequence (30-second TCP RST propagation lag) as an
explicit design constraint for any autoscaling solution.
Note: preStop hooks and terminationGracePeriodSeconds are insufficient; the correct
mitigation is scale-down suppression, not graceful drain duration extension.

Subsection 4.5: The Stabilization Window — Mechanism and Correctness
Without stabilization: 0 connections → clamped to minReplicas=2 → scale-down → reprovisioning lag on reconnect.
With 120s window: 0 connections appended, but window contains desired=8 entries from
preceding 120s → stabilized = max(W) = 8 → all 8 pods held warm.

Critical distinction from HPA's built-in window: HPA stabilizes metric-signal
recommendations (near-zero CPU → every entry recommends minReplicas → output =
minReplicas). This controller stabilizes connection-derived desired counts (retains highwater mark). Cannot be replicated by tuning HPA window duration.
Subsection 4.6: Controller Design and Stability Properties
Feedback loop framing: Observe → Compare → Act structure (mirrors HPA but with correct
signal and hysteresis)
Hysteresis mechanism: scaleDownCooldownSeconds as dead zone preventing oscillation
Convergence bound: Equation 3 — ⌈(current - desired) / maxScaleDownStep⌉
reconciliation cycles. Example: 8→2 with step=2 → 3 cycles ≈ 45 seconds.
Parameter selection and sensitivity: why targetConnsPerPod=100, maxScaleDownStep=2,
scaleDownCooldownSeconds=120 were chosen; sensitivity analysis.
Delay margin: scrape lag (15s) + reconcile period (15s) = 30s max observation lag << 120s
cooldown → lag fully absorbed.

Section 9: Discussion (Paper 2)
Length: ~600 words Draws from the original Discussion section. Adapts subsections to Paper
2's solution framing.
Subsection 9.1: The Correct Signal Changes Everything
Experiment C operated at CPU_WORK=0 throughout. A controller on the correct signal
(connections) outperforms a proportional controller on the wrong signal (CPU)
categorically, not just marginally.
Philosophical implication: architecture (signal choice + lifecycle semantics) matters more
than algorithm sophistication.
Subsection 9.2: Stabilization Semantics as a First-Class Design Dimension
Revisit the semantic distinction: HPA stabilizes CPU-derived replica recommendations;
StatefulAutoscaler stabilizes connection-derived desired counts.
This distinction explains why Experiment D (correct metric, HPA stabilization) succeeds
for the 90s DROP 1 but fails at efficiency: HPA's 300s window is semantically "dumb" — it
holds pods not because it knows connections are present but because 300s haven't elapsed.
StatefulAutoscaler holds pods because it knows connections were present.

Implication: for disconnection gaps > stabilizationWindowSeconds (Experiment D would
fail at gaps > 300s; StatefulAutoscaler would fail at gaps > 120s), controller-architecturelevel stabilization must be tuned to the expected disconnection gap distribution.
Subsection 9.3: Step-Rate Limiting as a Safety Mechanism
maxScaleUpStep and maxScaleDownStep: contribute to monotonic trajectories and avoid
explosive scaling events.
Particularly important: scale-down step limiting bounds the "blast radius" — maximum
connections destroyed by any single scale-down event = maxScaleDownStep ×
targetConnsPerPod.
Contrast with KEDA: no per-cycle step limit; once cooldown expires, HPA may remove
many pods in a single reconciliation.
Subsection 9.4: Generalizability Beyond WebSocket
The sole requirement for applying this controller to any protocol: a Prometheus gauge
reflecting current active connection population.
Direct applicability: gRPC streaming, MQTT (demonstrated), Server-Sent Events, database
connection pool managers.
MQTT validation confirms protocol-agnosticism. The active_connections abstraction is
protocol-neutral.
Subsection 9.5: Operational and Fairness Considerations
Connection-count scaling implicitly couples resource allocation to client session behavior.
Multi-tenant risk: high-connection users driving disproportionate scale-up.
Mitigation: namespace-scoped resource quotas, per-user connection limits at application
layer.

Length: ~500 words Include all limitations from the original that pertain to the
StatefulAutoscaler design and evaluation. Do NOT repeat Paper 1's limitations (evaluation

Include (adapted from original Section 12):
1. Evaluation environment: Single-machine Kind cluster; absolute timing values may differ
on multi-node cloud infrastructure.

2. Workload generalizability: Synthetic ramps and deterministic gaps; real-world bursty
traffic not yet validated. For stationary Poisson arrivals and gradual long-term drift:
bounded and predictable degradation modes described.
3. Observability dependency: scaleDownCooldownSeconds > scrape_interval invariant
must be maintained. Indefinitely long Prometheus outage → window expiry → scale-down
to minReplicas.
4. No predictive scaling: Reactive only. Rate-based proactive signal
( rate(new_connections_total[1m]) ) is outside current scope.
5. Drain semantics scope: Validated in MQTT-B only; WebSocket drain not yet tested end-toend.

7. Window boundary edge case: CYCLE 2 transient dip to 5–6 replicas; configurable
minimum hold-time following last scale-up would prevent this.
8. Per-pod granularity: WebSocket evaluation uses aggregate sum(active_connections) .
Hot-pod imbalance detection requires per-pod queries (demonstrated in MQTT-B;
WebSocket extension planned).
9. MQTT session redistribution mechanism: Drain-reconnect requires client-side retry logic.
Broker-level clustering would be more transparent.
10. MQTT connection-ramp bottleneck: 339/1,000 clients admitted — OS-level TCP ceiling,
not controller limitation. Pre-warming or retry-capable clients required.
11. MQTT memory instrumentation gap: memory.log collector recorded zeros; cpu.log
samples showed ~79Mi peak. Qualitative CPU-independence result unaffected; memory
conclusions are exploratory.
12. MQTT multi-run replication: MQTT-A and MQTT-B are single runs. Five-run statistical
replication (as for Experiments C–E) is future work.

Paper 1 Abstract Draft
The Kubernetes Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA) implements a proportional control law over
CPU utilization as its primary scaling signal — an architecture that performs reliably for
stateless, request-driven workloads but is structurally mismatched to stateful, persistentconnection protocols such as WebSocket. In WebSocket deployments, the primary unit of

computational commitment is an active network connection whose session state persists
independently of CPU activity; the termination of any pod immediately and irrecoverably severs
every connection it hosts. This paper presents a systematic empirical characterization of HPA
failure under WebSocket workloads through a progressive chain of four controlled experiments.
Experiment A establishes a correct-behavior baseline under the idealized condition of CPU-toconnection proportionality. Experiment B1 demonstrates that HPA over-provisions by 87.5%
under cyclic load, exhausting maxReplicas within 99 seconds and requiring 650 seconds to
recover. Experiment B2-Instrumented uses Prometheus instrumentation to measure
reconnection storm rates causally linked to HPA scale-down decisions, with a peak observed rate
of 1,400 connections/second and a 51.9% connection count overshoot (1,215 vs. 800 target).
Experiment B3 demonstrates permanent, irrecoverable staircase connection destruction under
non-reconnecting clients, with the active session population monotonically reduced from 800 to
79 with zero recovery. We establish that these failure modes are architectural rather than
configurational: they arise from the semantic invalidity of CPU as a scaling signal for persistentconnection workloads and cannot be remediated by HPA parameter tuning alone. The findings
motivate the design of connection-aware autoscaling policies as presented in the companion
paper.
Keywords: Kubernetes, Horizontal Pod Autoscaler, WebSocket, Stateful Workloads,
Reconnection Storm, Prometheus, Measurement Study, Connection-Aware Autoscaling

Paper 2 Abstract Draft
CPU-based Kubernetes HPA is empirically shown in the companion paper to exhibit three
structural failure modes for stateful WebSocket workloads: 87.5% over-provisioning,
reconnection storms reaching 1,400 connections/second, and permanent staircase connection
destruction. This paper presents the design, implementation, and multi-baseline experimental
validation of the StatefulAutoscaler — a custom Kubernetes controller built on the
Kubebuilder SDK that resolves all three failure modes by scaling exclusively on
sum(active_connections) queried from Prometheus and incorporating a sliding-window
stabilization mechanism ( scaleDownCooldownSeconds ) to suppress premature scale-down
during transient connection drops. Controlled experimental evaluation across a two-cycle
restorm simulation (Experiment C, five independent runs) demonstrates exact replica targeting
(⌈800/100⌉ = 8 replicas, 0% waste), zero connection loss across all observed scaling events, and
complete pod preservation through a 90-second disconnection gap via the stabilization window
(scale-down reaction time 119 ± 2s; pod-seconds 4,280 ± 67). Comparative baseline evaluation
against HPA augmented with a custom connection-count metric via the Prometheus Adapter
(Experiment D, five runs) and KEDA configured with a matching cooldownPeriod (Experiment
E, five runs) reveals a nuanced finding: all three connection-aware configurations preserve pods
through the 90-second DROP 1 gap, with the StatefulAutoscaler differentiating on scale-up
latency (22s vs. 54s for Experiment D) and final scale-down efficiency (119s vs. 313s). Three

adversarial failure scenarios establish the operational envelope: metric staleness degrades
gracefully to a reaction lag bounded by the scrape interval; instantaneous connection spikes
expose pod scheduling latency as the binding constraint; Prometheus unavailability triggers
safe-default hold behavior with no connection loss. MQTT generalization experiments confirm
protocol-agnosticism: the identical controller binary, with zero source-code changes, scales 44%
faster than CPU HPA (38s vs. 68s), preserves all 339 sessions during a forced scale-down
(maximum step-drop: 2 vs. 639-connection cliff), and redistributes sessions across pods via
drain-reconnect semantics.

11. Risk Flags and Edge Cases
Risk 1: The "flat bridge" narrative
The flat bridge during DROP 1 in Experiment C is the central empirical result of Paper 2. It must
be described with absolute precision: replicas held at exactly 8 for the full 90-second gap, while
connection count was exactly 0. Do not soften this or introduce ambiguity. The contrast with B3
(where connections are destroyed by this point) must be made explicit.
Risk 2: Experiment D's result was surprising
The initial hypothesis for Experiment D was that HPA with a custom metric would fail to
preserve pods through DROP 1. It did not fail — it preserved pods via the 300-second
stabilization window. The paper must present this honestly as a finding that refines the narrative:
metric choice alone is not the differentiator for the 90-second DROP 1 scenario; the
differentiators are scale-up latency and final scale-down efficiency. Do not hide or minimize this.
Risk 3: Run 1 of Experiment C
Run 1 is explicitly excluded from all statistics with a documented reason. This must be
mentioned in a dedicated paragraph (as in the original) and not swept under the rug. The
exclusion reason (persistent-replica test harness artefact, not controller behavior) must be stated
clearly.
Risk 4: MQTT ceiling at 339/1,000
Both MQTT-A and MQTT-B admitted 339 of 1,000 clients. This is not a controller failure — it is an
OS-level TCP stack limitation in the Kind environment. This must be clearly attributed and not
presented as a limitation of the StatefulAutoscaler . The Future Work section (Subsection 11.1)
addresses it directly.
Risk 5: Window boundary transient dip in Experiment C
During early CYCLE 2, a brief dip to 5–6 replicas was observed. This is a real behavior of the
controller that must be documented honestly (as in the original), not omitted. It is mentioned in
Experiment C (Subsection 5.5), acknowledged in Limitations (item 7), and addressed in Future
Work. The key point: no connection loss occurred during the dip.
Risk 6: MQTT memory instrumentation gap
The MQTT-B memory.log recorded zeros. This must be noted in Limitations (item 11) as an
instrumentation defect, and MQTT memory conclusions must be labeled as exploratory. The
cpu.log values (~79Mi observed via kubectl top) should be cited for completeness without
overstating their reliability.

Risk 7: Paper 2 Section ordering
The original paper has adversarial scenarios after baselines, MQTT after adversarial. This
ordering is preserved in Paper 2. Do not reorder sections without good reason — the logical flow
(design → primary validation → comparative baselines → failure bounds → generalization) is
intentional.
Risk 8: Dual GitHub repositories
Paper 1 cites https://github.com/AbhasBenevolentDictator/STAR (HPA experiments A, B1,
B2, B3). Paper 2 cites https://github.com/MistaHolmes/ws-k8-controller
(StatefulAutoscaler + Experiments C, D, E, MQTT). These must not be mixed up. Both must be
verified as publicly accessible before submission.
